## Detailed Explanation

People often want to describe a ring for practical reasons: to find something similar, to explain a preference to a partner, or to brief a jeweler for a custom design. Jewelers use fairly consistent vocabulary, and once you know the main parts of a ring, describing one becomes much easier.

### 1. The center stone shape

This is usually the first word in any description. Common shapes include round, oval, cushion, princess (square), emerald (rectangular with stepped facets), radiant, pear, marquise, Asscher and heart. If the stone has an unusual cut, describe what you see: elongated, rounded corners, pointed at one end.

### 2. The setting

The setting is how the stone is held:

- **Prong:** metal claws hold the stone. Note how many prongs and their shape (rounded, pointed, claw).
- **Bezel:** a rim of metal wraps around the stone's edge, giving a smooth, modern look.
- **Halo:** a ring of small stones surrounds the center stone.
- **Hidden halo:** small stones sit under the center stone and are visible mainly from the side.
- **Cathedral:** metal arches rise from the band to hold the stone higher.
- **Basket or peg head:** the structure under the stone, visible from the side.

### 3. The band

Describe the band's style and metal:

- **Style:** plain or polished, pavé (lined with tiny stones), channel-set, split shank, twisted, knife-edge, tapered.
- **Width:** thin, medium or wide is usually enough.
- **Metal:** yellow gold, white gold, rose gold or platinum. Two-tone rings use different metals for the band and setting.

### 4. Side stones and details

Mention any extra features: three-stone designs, small side stones, milgrain (tiny beaded edges), engraving, or a vintage-style filigree pattern.

### 5. The overall style

Finish with a general label if one fits: classic solitaire, vintage-inspired, minimalist, art deco, east-west (stone set sideways), toi et moi (two stones side by side). These help when searching or browsing.

### Putting it together

A complete description might read: "Oval center stone, four-prong setting with a hidden halo, thin pavé band in platinum." That's short, specific and easy for someone else to picture.

### What a photo can't tell you

Photos show style, not quality. Stone size, color, clarity and whether a stone is natural, lab-grown or a simulant can't be judged reliably from a picture. A jeweler can examine the ring or the paperwork if those details matter.

## Common Scenarios

- **You want something similar to a ring you saw online.** Use the description terms above when searching, and save the photo to show a jeweler.
- **You're dropping hints to a partner.** A one-line description plus a couple of screenshots is clearer than "something sparkly."
- **You're designing a custom ring.** Bring descriptions of the parts you like from different rings, for example the band from one and the setting from another.
- **You've inherited a ring and want to know more.** A jeweler or appraiser can identify the metal, stones and possibly its era.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the most important part of a ring description?**  
The stone shape and the setting. Those two details shape the overall look more than anything else.

**How do I tell white gold from platinum in a photo?**  
You usually can't. A jeweler can check hallmarks inside the band or test the metal.

**Can I tell the carat weight from a photo?**  
Not reliably. Finger size, camera angle and setting style all change how big a stone looks.

**What does "solitaire" mean?**  
A ring with a single center stone and no other prominent stones, though some solitaires have a lightly decorated band.
